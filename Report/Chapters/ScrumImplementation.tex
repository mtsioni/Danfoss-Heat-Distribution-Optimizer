The project was developed on the principle of the Agile methodology, implemented through the Scrum framework. 
Agile software development is founded on the general principle of prioritising people, behaviours, and collaboration among team members rather than adhering to rigid processes, tools, and contracts. \cite{Adi2015}. 
By its nature, Agile is designed to provide a fast, lightweight and alerting environment \cite{Adi2015}. Agile can increase speed and flexibility while enabling iterative development and frequent product increments \cite{Adi2015}, when it is combined with the Scrum framework. Scrum is a holistic and flexible development framework following Agile principles.

As this semester’s project requirements were clearly defined from the beginning, implementing the assignment using the Scrum framework was straightforward and offered both academic and practical experience. 
Experience, that was valuable enough to create a solid grounding for each member’s professional competencies; as the knowledge acquired by working daily with the scrum framework proved to be educational in many aspects, which will be further analyzed later. The team which is consisted of six members. Despite the small number of team members, it is consistent with case studies which state that small teams are utilized to provide maximized performance \cite{Hoegl2005} in a project. 

The team implemented the Scrum Roles as early as the Sprint 1. 
Specifically, the role of the Scrum Master (SM) was decided to be taken in rotation by each member per Sprint. 
All the members were part of the Development Team (DT) which contributed in both programing and reviewing/testing. The role of the Product Owner (PO) in the team, was given to the team’s Teacher Assistant, for the first two to three sprints. As the team members became more familiar with the Product Requirements, it was decided by consensus to assign the role of PO to the team member with the most thorough and comprehensive understanding of the requirements. Additionally, the team assigned a different coordinator in every Sprint, for the various meetings and ceremonies held within a Sprint.

The team decided to follow the recommended four to six Sprints \cite{Hron2022} from the beginning of the project. The team agreed to keep the Sprint period at two weeks intervals. In the case of extraordinary circumstances, the Sprint period could be longer. However, as demonstrated at the end of the project, the group conducted six Sprints in total, each lasting two weeks, which concluded in the last week of May.

The industry proposed Sprint ceremonies were reverently followed by the team; Sprint Plannings, 
weekly Stand-up meetings, Sprint Reviews and Sprint Retrospectives \cite{Adi2015}. 
Each Sprint began with a thorough Sprint Planning. 
In the beginning of the project, it was inevitable for the team to not stable upon organizational mistakes, while the group members were still in the learning process of managing the different Sprint events. For example, due to lack of detailed Sprint Planning in the beginning, incidents like adding tickets mid-Sprint were unavoidable. Despite that, with the help of constructive feedback by the lecturers and intragroup Retrospectives, the team managed to create consecutive thorough Sprint Plannings.

The Sprint workflow continued with weekly Stand-up meetings, which were minimum two in every Sprint. 
The reason being that the group, while still in the process of learning how to handle the workload of the project, were meeting more frequently than normal. As it is being stated in the literature \cite{Hoegl2005}, the stand-up meetings of the team were, in fact, short and informative sessions about each other’s work that week. 
The team decided that weekly Stand-up meetings were more efficient for the scale and purpose of this project, rather than daily ones. Due to pair-programing, teams of two or three people met more often to complete their Sprint tasks. On other occasions, the full team would meet for organizational purposes, review and testing sessions, or to solve problems requiring everyone's attention. 
In general, the meetings, as well as the pair-programing and reviewing/testing sessions, were held mostly online through platforms like Discord. 

The Sprint Reviews and Sprint Retrospectives proved to be fruitful for the team. 
In those ceremonies many issues, incidents as well as productive ideas were raised. 
As expected from a bachelor-level project group, behavioral issues, schedule conflicts and misaligned work habits were not possible to avoid. Specifically, the most friction between members occured during Sprint 3.
For that reason, the group implemented the START/STOP/CONTINUE method during the Retrospective, 
contacted with “silent writing” and “round-robin sharing” methods. 
This procedure provided transparency to emotions and grievances, as well as opportunities to open-u;  as it offered empowerment and psychological safety. 
Thus, those ceremonies turned out to be tremendously important for the group, 
as through them the team was called to face both their individual and collective shortcomings. 
Resulting in solving these problems more maturely in personal and professional level than before. 

The processes of defining the Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog were straightforward. 
The Product Backlog refinement of the requirements was slightly more challenging as a procedure for the group, than the refinement of requirements for the Sprint Backlog. 
The progression of understanding the requirements and solidifying them into concrete 
high-level, technical, functional and non-functional requirements was time consuming and complex. Therefore, the team spent two whole Sprints to finalize them and formulate them into UML diagrams, which in result became the backbone of the whole code of the project. 
The team chose not to format the requirements into user stories or use cases. Instead, it was more efficient for the team members to divide the requirements into explicit technical tickets, as they offered clear and actionable coding steps than what abstract scenarios would provide.

Once the Product Backlog requirements were clearly defined, the Sprint Backlog refinement for Sprints 1-4 was straightforward. 
The reason being that those Sprints were clearly technical ones and the work items for those were clearly defined. 
There were also occurrences where the Product Backlog would be emptied and then the team would need to add more tickets in the Product Backlog or define more specific ones, but that was not the norm for the group. In other cases, incomplete tasks from previous Sprints would be transferred back to the Product Backlog, where then the team in one of the Sprint Planning meetings would decide which of the incomplete tasks would move to the next Sprint or would stay there until they become relevant in a future Sprint.

Jira was selected by the team as the tool to implement the Scrum framework, primarily because it is widely recognized as an industry standard. The team chose to use it in order to gain practical experience and master the software while studying and before entering the professional workforce after graduating.
For each ticket on Jira, the group used their own developed story points system. 
Specifically, the team chose on a 10-point scale to represent the sum of difficulty and importance of a task. Five points for importance and five points for difficulty. The addition of those two would give the total sum of story points of a specific ticket. 
Alongside that, each member had to include the estimated time and the actual time spent on a ticket, 
as well as add the sub-team they were belonging to, in case that ticket was part of a pair-programing session.

The team understood that story points, time estimation and time spent on a ticket were essential for another tool of Scrum called the burnout charts \cite{Hron2022}. 
The team did not clearly understand in the first two Sprints the importance and usefulness of the burnout charts, which can track the productivity of the team by measuring task velocity \cite{Hron2022}. 
If the usefulness of burnout charts had been clearly understood from the beginning, it would have been possible to avoid adding tickets mid-Sprint. Monitoring productivity and progress on the burnout chart would have helped the team plan future work more efficiently \cite{Hron2022} from the early stages of developing the project.

As presented in this chapter, deviations from the textbook Scrum framework were inevitable in certain cases and purposeful in others. 
While various deviations occured and explained beforehand, the team agreed these instances were clearly justified by two main factors: either ‘Context’ or ‘Performance’ \cite{Hron2022}. As defined by the source, adaptations such as the rotation of the Scrum Master role each Sprint and holding weekly rather than daily Stand-ups are categorized as ‘Context’ \cite{Hron2022}. Due to the academic nature of this project, adapting standard Scrum roles and schedules to student life was unavoidable.
Other deviations, such as the customized story point system and holding extra weekly meetings alongside the Scrum ceremonies are categorized as ‘Performance’ deviations by the literature \cite{Hron2022}. In these case, limited time made the team deviate from how Scrum is traditionally carried out in an industry setting.

As highlighted in the literature, following all the Scrum events positevily influences the perception of being successful at Scrum \cite{Kadenic2023}. However, to complement this idea, it is worth mentioning that by following all the Scrum events, the group achieved a sense of success that went beyond professional milestones. Following these events allowed the team members to mature as individuals who learn from personal mistakes, adapt to challenges, and support continuous learning and improvement \cite{Hron2022}.
